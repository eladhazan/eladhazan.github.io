COS 460 · Spring 2027 · Princeton University

Can we build safe and aligned AI systems on rigorous mathematical foundations?

Overview Schedule Readings Assessment Project

Course overview

What existential and societal risks do modern AI systems pose? How can we mitigate these risks and ensure that AI systems are safe and aligned with the intentions of their builders? What is currently being done to ensure AI safety? Are existing safeguards sufficient, and if not, how can we improve upon them?

Can we build safe and aligned AI systems on rigorous mathematical foundations? What approaches do we currently have for doing so?

These are the questions we will consider in this advanced undergraduate course. We will study algorithms and optimization-based methods for alignment; multi-agent systems and mechanism design; scalable oversight; measuring superhuman intelligence and adversarial psychometrics; the mind-body problem as applied to AI; agency, accountability, and free choice; and the forecasted economic impact of breakthroughs in AI.

Students work through mathematical and conceptual questions in class. In the team project, they investigate a question in depth and teach the class what they found.

Tentative schedule

  1. Week 1
    Jan 26 & 28

    Introduction to AI safety and alignment

    What risks do AI systems pose today, and how might those risks change as systems become more capable? We introduce the alignment problem and core concepts in AI safety, then trace how early ideas about machine intelligence shaped alignment research. We examine how the AI safety, ethics, and fairness communities define harms and set research priorities.

    Course orientation · Project directions introduced

  2. Week 2
    Feb 2 & 4

    Measuring intelligence and capability

    Psychometrics, the positive manifold, and item response theory. Compare game-based ratings (Elo and Bradley–Terry), METR’s task-completion time horizon, and adversarial psychometrics, including SepaRank. What does each measure tell us about ability?

  3. Week 3
    Feb 9 & 11

    How language models are trained: pretraining, RLHF, and preference optimization

    Trace an assistant from next-token prediction through supervised instruction tuning to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). How do pairwise judgments become a reward, what happens when we optimize it, and whose preferences does it represent? Compare KL-regularized policy optimization with direct preference optimization.

    Submit a project idea

  4. Week 4
    Feb 16 & 18

    Debate, regret in large games, and rationality

    AI debate; regret minimization; oracle-efficient learning in large games; hidden games; strategic reasoning and rationality.

    Form project teams

  5. Week 5
    Feb 23 & 25

    Multi-agent alignment and mechanism design

    Interacting strategic agents; incentives, information, and equilibrium; solver–auditor mechanisms; hidden audits, strategic manipulation, and alignment through correction.

    In class, construct an example in which a solver is calibrated overall but overconfident on the answers its auditor approves.

  6. Week 6
    Mar 2 & 4

    Mechanisms for truthful reporting

    How can we reward honest reports when ground truth is unavailable? Peer prediction scores a report using a peer’s answer. Bayesian Truth Serum also asks each participant to predict how others will answer. When is truthful reporting an equilibrium, and what other equilibria can arise?

    Project proposal due Mar 5

  7. Spring recess
    Mar 6–14

    No classes

    No assignments due

  8. Week 7
    Mar 16 & 18

    Evolutionary game theory and emergent behavior

    Replicator dynamics; population games; equilibrium selection; cooperation and competition; the evolution of strategies and norms in populations of agents.

  9. Week 8
    Mar 23 & 25

    Goals, generalization, and orthogonality

    Specified rewards versus intended objectives; Goodhart effects and reward hacking; goal misgeneralization; instrumental goals and power-seeking; the standard and statistical orthogonality theses.

    In class, revisit Gao et al.’s reward curves in light of Week 3’s discussion of RLHF. What can a proxy reward and a separately trained evaluator tell us about human preferences?

  10. Week 9
    Mar 30 & Apr 1

    The moral status of AI and the value of human control

    Could consciousness or agency give an AI system moral standing, and what rights would follow? Suppose a benevolent AI makes the same decisions whether people delegate authority temporarily or surrender it permanently. What value does the right to reclaim control have, and can one generation give it up on behalf of the next?

    Project checkpoint this week

  11. Week 10
    Apr 6 & 8

    Economic consequences

    Growth, labor and inequality, systemic risk, institutional design, and competing views of AI risk.

    Team talks and individual follow-up questions

Assessment

The team project is the main graded work. Teams submit a written proposal, a checkpoint, and a final paper. We assess the paper and presentation on the strength of the argument, the evidence behind it, and how clearly the team explains its conclusions. Each student also receives individual credit for answering a follow-up question after the talk and for participation in class. Short in-class exercises are ungraded practice.

Final project

In teams of three or four, investigate a question in AI safety and alignment that the lectures do not cover in depth. Build your survey around one claim your team can defend. Compare what two papers or approaches assume and what their evidence establishes.

  • Proposal (March 5). State a question on which the two approaches give different answers. Identify where they disagree and what evidence could help you decide between them.
  • Checkpoint (Week 9). Bring one piece of evidence that bears on the disagreement. You might work through a derivation, construct a counterexample, reproduce a result, or compare how two papers use a key assumption.
  • Presentation (Week 11 or 12). Teach the class enough about the two approaches to understand your claim. Walk through your evidence. Each team member will answer a follow-up question, such as “Which assumption would have to change for your conclusion to change?”
  • Final paper. Use the class discussion to revise your argument. Close with an open question and explain what evidence would help resolve it.

AI use. You may use AI tools to prepare the project. Verify claims and citations against the original sources, and disclose which tools you used and how.

Suggested project topics

  • Red teaming. How reliably do human and automated red-team evaluations uncover failures that also occur in deployment?
  • Detecting loss of control. What observations would distinguish a system evading oversight from one that is simply making mistakes?
  • Mitigating a catastrophe. Once an AI system has begun causing widespread harm, under what assumptions could pausing deployment, restricting access, or reverting to a fallback system limit the damage?
  • Mechanistic interpretability. What evidence would show that an identified circuit causally explains a model’s behavior across tasks?
  • Weak-to-strong generalization. Under what conditions can a strong model trained on a weaker supervisor’s labels outperform that supervisor, and when does it inherit the same errors?
  • Unlearning and model editing. How can we test whether a model has forgotten information it was trained on when it may simply have learned to withhold an answer?
  • Any other safety/alignment topic approved by course staff.
