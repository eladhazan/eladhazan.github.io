COS 460 · Spring 2027 · Princeton University

How can we build and evaluate AI systems whose capabilities may approach or exceed those of their human designers?

Course overview

This advanced undergraduate course develops technical foundations for AI safety and alignment through machine learning, algorithms, optimization, game theory, control, scalable oversight, adversarial evaluation, and mechanism design.

We study advanced AI both as a learner and as part of a system of interacting agents: solvers, auditors, proposers, judges, and human overseers. Their incentives and information shape the outcome. Philosophical, ethical, and economic questions provide context, while the central emphasis remains technical and mathematical.

By the end of the course, students should be able to formulate evaluation and alignment problems mathematically, analyze incentives and strategic behavior, apply tools from learning and game theory, critically assess risk arguments, and develop an original project with clear assumptions, evidence, and limitations.

Problem sets combine technical exercises, short proofs, and focused computational work. Reading responses are submitted before designated class meetings. Assignment handouts and submission instructions will be posted before release.

Team project

Teams of three or four will develop a concrete theorem, protocol, implementation, or careful empirical result. Possible directions include:

  • Reproducing or extending model-generated evaluation and SepaRank.
  • Designing solver–auditor or debate mechanisms.
  • Analyzing incentives, equilibria, and strategic manipulation.
  • Evaluating weak-to-strong oversight.
  • Building bounded red-team and correction pipelines.

Every project must include a reproducibility statement, a description of each team member’s contribution, and disclosure of generative-AI assistance. The final deliverable is a paper with a reproducible artifact or theory appendix.

Course policies

Generative AI and academic integrity

Generative AI may be used for brainstorming, coding assistance, editing, and experiments when an assignment permits it. Students remain responsible for every claim, proof, citation, and line of submitted code. All use must be disclosed, including the tool, purpose, and substantive contribution. Some assignments may prohibit generative-AI assistance to assess individual understanding.

Problem-set solutions and reading responses must be written independently unless an assignment states otherwise, and all collaborators must be acknowledged. Project teams must identify each member’s contribution. Students must follow Princeton’s Rights, Rules, Responsibilities and cite all sources, data, models, and external code.

Attendance, late work, and reasonable exceptions

Attendance and prepared participation are expected. Notify the instructor or teaching staff when illness, religious observance, disability-related needs, varsity athletics, or serious personal circumstances interfere with attendance or deadlines. Reasonable exceptions and extensions will be granted when appropriate.

Each student has three 24-hour late tokens for problem sets or reading responses; no more than two tokens may be used on one assignment. Beyond these tokens, work loses 10 percent per day unless an extension has been approved. Term-time work cannot be accepted after Dean’s Date, May 4, 2027.

Accessibility

Students who need accommodations should work with the Office of Disability Services and contact the instructor early in the term. Course materials will be provided in accessible formats. Students are encouraged to discuss barriers to participation.
