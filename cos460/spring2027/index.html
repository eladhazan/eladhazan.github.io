Course overview

What existential and societal risks do modern AI systems pose? How can we mitigate these risks and ensure that AI systems are safe and aligned with the intentions of their builders? What is currently being done to ensure AI safety? Are existing safeguards sufficient, and if not, how can we improve upon them?

Can we build safe and aligned AI systems on rigorous mathematical foundations? What approaches do we currently have for doing so?

These are the questions we will consider in this advanced undergraduate course. We will study algorithms and optimization-based methods for alignment; multi-agent systems and mechanism design; scalable oversight; measuring superhuman intelligence and adversarial psychometrics; the mind-body problem as applied to AI; agency, accountability, and free choice; and the forecasted economic impact of breakthroughs in AI.

The course will be both broad and rigorous, and students will be expected to engage in both conceptual and rigorous mathematical thinking.

Tentative schedule

  1. Week 1
    Jan 25–29

    Introduction, history, and survey of AI safety and alignment

    The control and alignment problems; the history of AI safety; superintelligence and intelligence explosion; major risk arguments; a survey of technical approaches.

    Course orientation · Project directions introduced

  2. Week 2
    Feb 1–5

    Evaluating intelligence beyond human scale

    Capabilities and limits of human evaluation; benchmark saturation; adversarial psychometrics; model-generated evaluation; SepaRank and separating questions.

    Problem Set 1 assigned

  3. Week 3
    Feb 8–12

    Debate, regret in large games, and rationality

    AI debate; regret minimization; oracle-efficient learning in large games; hidden games; strategic reasoning and rationality.

    Problem Set 1 due Feb 12 · Project idea submission

  4. Week 4
    Feb 15–19

    Multi-agent alignment and mechanism design

    Interacting strategic agents; incentives, information, and equilibrium; solver–auditor mechanisms; hidden audits, strategic manipulation, and alignment through correction.

    Problem Set 2 assigned · Project teams formed

  5. Week 5
    Feb 22–26

    Evolutionary game theory and emergent behavior

    Replicator dynamics; population games; equilibrium selection; cooperation and competition; the evolution of strategies and norms in populations of agents.

    Problem Set 2 due Feb 26

  6. Week 6
    Mar 1–5

    RLHF and preference optimization

    Reinforcement learning and contextual-bandit foundations; preference learning and reward modeling; the RLHF pipeline; KL-regularized policy optimization; direct preference optimization; reward-model overoptimization.

    Project proposal due Mar 5

  7. Spring recess
    Mar 6–14

    No classes

    No assignments due

  8. Week 7
    Mar 15–19

    Adversarial robustness, interpretability, and deception

    Threat models, jailbreaks, red teaming, latent knowledge, interpretability, deceptive behavior, deployment monitoring, defense in depth, and alignment faking.

  9. Week 8
    Mar 22–26

    Specified rewards versus intended objectives; Goodhart effects and reward hacking; goal misgeneralization; instrumental goals and power-seeking; the standard and statistical orthogonality theses.

    Problem Set 3 due Mar 26 · Problem Set 4 assigned

  10. Week 9
    Mar 29–Apr 2

    Consciousness, moral status, agency, free choice, responsibility gaps, and accountability for autonomous systems.

  11. Week 10

Assessment

The course grade combines course participation, written exercises, a final paper, and a final project presentation.

Team project

Students work in teams of three or four on a semester project involving theory, empirical evaluation, or mechanism design. Each team will give an oral presentation of its project and submit a final paper.
